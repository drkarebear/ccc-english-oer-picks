Transparency & Methodology

General Scoring Method

How CCC English OER Picks turns course requirements into practical, course-specific alignment observations—and what those scores do and do not mean. Every resource passes through two independent lenses, course alignment (COR) and the ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework, each reviewed by AI first and then by a human editor.

What the scores are for

The alignment scores are designed to help faculty quickly compare a large number of open educational resources before investing time opening, reading, and evaluating each one in depth.

They are not rankings of overall quality, and they are not official determinations that an OER satisfies a Common Course Numbering (CCN) course, a local course outline, articulation requirements, or institutional curriculum standards.

Instead, each score answers a narrower question: How much meaningful, teachable coverage of this particular course criterion appears to be present in this resource?

The four-point alignment scale

Every course-specific rubric uses the same general scale. The individual criteria change from course to course, but the meaning of the dots stays consistent.

●●● Strong alignment

The resource provides substantial, teachable coverage of this criterion and could reasonably support a major part of the course requirement.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

The resource provides useful coverage, but faculty may want additional readings, examples, assignments, or instruction.

●○○ Limited alignment

The resource touches the criterion but is better understood as supplemental rather than substantial coverage.

○○○ Not a significant focus

The criterion is absent, minimal, or outside the intended purpose of the resource.

Three dots does not mean “best book.” A resource can be excellent at a narrower job and still earn one or zero dots for criteria it was never designed to address.

Scores are course-specific

The same OER may receive very different observations in C1000, C1001, C1002, and C1003 because those courses ask students and instructors to do different work. The goal is not to assign one permanent score to a book. The goal is to consider how well that resource appears to serve a particular course.

Coverage, not prestige

Scores describe apparent coverage of a criterion. They do not measure reputation, popularity, production quality, or whether a faculty member personally likes the resource.

Role matters

A resource may be a strong primary text, a reader or anthology, or a targeted supplement. Not every OER needs to function as an all-in-one textbook.

Combinations may be stronger

When one resource has clear strengths and clear gaps, pairing it with another OER may be more useful than searching for a single text that does everything.

Scores can change

OER are revised, links change, and faculty notice things that an initial review may miss. Alignment observations are therefore treated as revisable.

How each review is produced: two lenses, four stages

Every resource goes through the same four-stage pipeline before anything is published: an AI-assisted pass against the course rubric (COR), a human editorial pass on that result, an AI-assisted pass against the ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework, and a human editorial pass on that result. The two lenses run one after the other, but their findings are never merged into a single score—see why editorial alignment and faculty experience stay separate below, and the IDEA crosswalk methodology for how that second lens is applied.

Preparation, before stage 1: each course's rubric is derived from statewide CCN expectations, and publicly available information about the OER—text, table of contents, chapter structure, assignments, ancillary materials, and licensing—is gathered for review.

Stage 1 · COR lens

AI-assisted pass against the course rubric

The OER is compared against the course-specific alignment rubric using an AI-assisted first pass, producing draft dot scores and supporting notes for every criterion in that course's rubric.

Stage 2 · COR lens

Human review of the course-alignment pass

A human editor checks the AI's draft scores against the resource itself, adjusts anything that seems off, and adds practical notes about likely strengths, gaps, and possible roles before this lens is published.

Stage 3 · IDEA lens

AI-assisted pass against the ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework

The same OER is separately reviewed with an AI-assisted pass against the ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework, asking a different set of questions about inclusion, diversity, equity, and anti-racism rather than course content.

Stage 4 · IDEA lens

Human review of the IDEA pass

A human editor reviews the AI's IDEA-framework observations the same way—checking them against the resource and adjusting as needed—and makes clear that this crosswalk is independent, not an official ASCCC OERI IDEA assessment or badge.

Important: AI-assisted analysis can miss context, misunderstand how a resource is intended to be used, or overlook material embedded in a text. That is true for both lenses, which is why every AI-assisted pass—COR and IDEA alike—is followed by a human editorial pass before anything is published.
A third, independent form of evidence sits outside this pipeline. Faculty recommendations and teaching notes are gathered separately from both lenses and can confirm, complicate, or correct what the four-stage review produced. See editorial alignment and faculty experience are different evidence below.

What the other labels mean

Several editorial labels appear beside the dot scores. These are practical shortcuts, not additional numerical ratings.

Start Here

A resource that appears especially worth investigating early when faculty are deciding where to begin. It is an editorial shortcut, not an award or official endorsement.

Best Role

A practical judgment about how the resource may work best—for example, as a primary or core text, a reader or anthology, or a targeted supplement.

Watch For

A note about something faculty may want to examine more closely, such as a coverage gap, mixed licensing, an older version, representation concerns, or a likely need for supplementation.

Editorial alignment and faculty experience are different evidence

The site intentionally does not blend the initial alignment observations with faculty recommendations into a single score.

Editorial alignment

Asks: What does this resource appear to cover in relation to this course? It is based on the course-specific rubric and review process.

Faculty experience

Asks: What happened when someone actually taught with it? Faculty can add recommendations and teaching notes based on real classroom use.

Keeping those two forms of evidence visible and separate allows faculty to see when they reinforce one another—and when lived teaching experience complicates the initial analysis.

What these scores should not be used for

These observations should not be treated as official CCN compliance reviews, curriculum approvals, articulation decisions, accessibility certifications, licensing audits, or endorsements by ASCCC, ASCCC OERI, or the California Community Colleges Chancellor's Office.

Faculty remain responsible for reviewing resources in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, student needs, accessibility expectations, and instructional goals.

See the course-specific rubrics

The general scoring scale stays consistent across the site, but each course uses different criteria. Course-specific rubric pages will explain exactly what is being considered for each course.
