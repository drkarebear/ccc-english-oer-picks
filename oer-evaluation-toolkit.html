FACULTY TOOLKIT · OER EVALUATION

Start with the course.
End with the evidence.

Use these tools to build or adapt a transparent OER evaluation process for your own course.

What this toolkit is for

This toolkit helps faculty move from a course document to a defensible OER decision. AI can help organize the work, but faculty remain responsible for the criteria, the evidence, and the teaching decision.

1. COURSEDefine what the course actually requires.
2. RUBRICBuild or adapt transparent criteria.
3. OERInvestigate the resource itself.
4. EVIDENCEVerify every important judgment.
5. DECISIONChoose the role the OER can play.

Prompt 1: Create a rubric from a COR, CCN, or C-ID record

This prompt is designed to turn a course document into a draft OER-evaluation rubric without pretending the AI is the curriculum expert.

BUILD THE RUBRIC

Course-document → OER rubric prompt

You are helping a college faculty member create a transparent rubric for evaluating whether an open educational resource (OER) is a good fit for a specific course.

I will provide a course document such as a Course Outline of Record (COR), Common Course Numbering (CCN) descriptor, or C-ID descriptor.

Your job is to create a DRAFT OER-evaluation rubric grounded only in the course document I provide.

Please follow these rules:

1. Extract the major knowledge areas, practices, skills, and learning expectations that an OER would need to support.
2. Group overlapping requirements into a manageable set of rubric categories rather than turning every sentence into a separate criterion.
3. Distinguish between:
   - essential course content or practices,
   - important but secondary areas,
   - and requirements that are not appropriate for evaluating a textbook or OER itself.
4. Do not invent course requirements that are not supported by the document.
5. For each rubric category, write a short description of what meaningful, teachable OER coverage would look like.
6. Create four performance levels:
   - Strong alignment
   - Meaningful alignment
   - Limited alignment
   - Not a significant focus
7. Make the levels describe the quality and teachability of coverage, not the prestige or overall quality of the OER.
8. Do not assign weights or produce a total score unless I ask for one.
9. Flag any criterion that is ambiguous and needs faculty judgment.
10. End with a short section titled "Faculty Review Needed" that identifies decisions the AI should not make on its own.

Output the rubric as a table with these columns:
- Rubric category
- What this category means for this course
- Strong alignment
- Meaningful alignment
- Limited alignment
- Not a significant focus

After the table, include:
- Course requirements that were intentionally NOT turned into OER criteria
- Faculty Review Needed
- A one-paragraph explanation of how you derived the categories from the course document

[PASTE COR, CCN, OR C-ID TEXT HERE]
What faculty should check: Are the categories faithful to the course document? Did the AI combine things that should stay separate? Did it turn instructor responsibilities, assessment requirements, or local policies into textbook criteria when they do not belong there?

Prompt 2: Evaluate an OER against the rubric

Once the rubric is stable, use this prompt to examine a specific OER. The key rule is that every rating needs inspectable evidence.

EVALUATE THE OER

Evidence-based OER review prompt

You are conducting a transparent OER alignment review for a college course.

I will provide:
1. an OER-evaluation rubric, and
2. an OER or available evidence from that OER.

Evaluate the OER criterion by criterion.

Rules:
1. Use only evidence available in the OER materials I provide or that you can directly inspect.
2. For every rating, identify the evidence that supports it: chapter, section, assignment, example, table of contents entry, ancillary, or other identifiable feature.
3. Do not infer that a topic is absent simply because it was not easy to find.
4. If the available material is incomplete, use "Insufficient evidence / uncertain" rather than guessing.
5. Distinguish between:
   - substantial, teachable coverage,
   - brief or partial mention,
   - and material that would require significant instructor supplementation.
6. Consider the intended role of the resource. A targeted supplement should not be penalized for failing to function like a complete textbook.
7. Do not produce an overall quality ranking.
8. Do not claim that the resource officially satisfies articulation, local curriculum, CCN, or C-ID requirements.
9. Separate factual evidence from interpretive judgment.

For each rubric category, output:
- Rating
- Evidence found
- Why the evidence supports the rating
- What faculty may still need to supplement
- Confidence level: High / Moderate / Low

At the end, provide:
- Strongest likely role for this OER:
  Primary course text / Core text plus supplements / Reader or anthology / Targeted supplement / Ancillary / Not a strong fit
- Major strengths
- Major gaps or uncertainties
- Evidence that should be checked by a faculty reviewer

Prompt 3: Audit an AI-generated OER review

This is the skeptical second-review prompt. It is meant to catch overclaiming, weak evidence, and false certainty.

AUDIT THE EVIDENCE

Skeptical second-review prompt

Review this OER assessment as a skeptical second reviewer.

Do not simply repeat or polish the existing analysis.

Identify:
1. ratings where the cited evidence may be too weak for the conclusion,
2. places where absence of evidence may have been mistaken for evidence of absence,
3. claims that appear to go beyond the material actually reviewed,
4. criteria where the intended role of the OER may have affected the rating,
5. ratings that should be changed to "Insufficient evidence / uncertain,"
6. places where the same evidence could reasonably support a different interpretation,
7. important OER sections or ancillaries that should be checked before finalizing the review.

For each concern, provide:
- Original claim or rating
- Why it may be overstated or uncertain
- What evidence would strengthen or correct it
- Recommended action: Keep / Revise / Mark uncertain / Investigate further

End with:
- Most trustworthy findings
- Least trustworthy findings
- Highest-priority items for faculty verification

Optional second lens: IDEA Framework crosswalk

Keep IDEA separate from course alignment. Course alignment asks whether the OER supports the content and practices of the course. An IDEA crosswalk asks different questions about inclusion, diversity, equity, and anti-racism.

When using the ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework, reference the framework explicitly and make clear that an independent crosswalk is not an official OERI IDEA assessment or badge.

Evidence rules worth keeping visible

AI is not the evidence.

A rating should point to something a faculty member can inspect.

Partial access means partial confidence.

A table of contents or preview cannot support the same certainty as a full-text review.

Not found ≠ not present.

Use “insufficient evidence” when the available material does not justify a conclusion.

Role matters.

A supplement should be judged as a supplement, not as a failed comprehensive textbook.

Keep facts and judgments separate.

Show what the OER contains before explaining what that means for the course.

Faculty make the adoption decision.

The method should support judgment, not replace it.

End with a teaching decision, not a leaderboard

A useful evaluation should help faculty decide what role a resource can play.

Primary course text

Strong enough across the major course needs to anchor the course.

Core text + supplements

Strong overall, with identifiable areas that need additional material.

Reader / anthology

Especially useful for readings, examples, or source material.

Targeted supplement

Excellent for one or more specific course needs.

Ancillary

Best used for exercises, assignments, practice, or support materials.

Not a strong fit

The available evidence suggests another resource would better serve this course or purpose.

A note on transparency

These prompts are starting points, not standards. Faculty should revise them for disciplinary context and publish the criteria they actually use. If the process changes, the method should change visibly too.
