ENGL C1003 Content Rubric · Content Lens

Critical Thinking and Writing through Literature

The project-developed content rubric used to examine how well an OER supports the literary, argumentative, reasoning, research, and writing work of ENGL C1003.

What this content rubric does

This rubric is built around the distinctive combination at the center of C1003: sustained literary study plus explicit critical-thinking and argumentative writing instruction. The categories are scored independently because a literature-centered course spine may need a focused logic or research companion, while a strong critical-thinking text may need substantial literary readings.

No single overall score. High alignment in reasoning or biases and fallacies requires explicit instruction—not merely opportunities to practice those skills while discussing literature.

This is the content lens. It asks whether an OER provides meaningful, teachable coverage of C1003's literary and critical-thinking work. It is separate from the site's ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework crosswalk, which examines inclusion, diversity, equity, and anti-racism. The two lenses are never merged into one score.

●●○

●○○
Limited alignment

○○○
Not a significant focus

How this content rubric was built

1. Preserve the course's dual emphasis.

The rubric keeps literary study and explicit critical-thinking instruction visible as separate but connected parts of C1003 rather than letting one stand in for the other.

2. Evaluate instruction, not opportunity.

Students may analyze, compare, or argue about literature without receiving explicit instruction in reasoning, fallacies, thesis development, or research. Stronger ratings require that instruction to be taught, modeled, or scaffolded.

3. Keep the evidence visible.

Reviewers support each rating with a brief rationale and a specific location—chapter, section, page, heading, or stable URL anchor whenever possible.

Combinations may be especially useful in C1003. A literature-centered OER with strong literary breadth and analysis may pair well with a focused critical-thinking, logic, research, or writing companion. The rubric is designed to make those complementary roles visible.
Working rubric: This is a faculty-developed analytical tool, not an official CCC, CCN, articulation, or curriculum determination. The rubric may be revised as evidence and faculty feedback improve the project.

The C1003 content criteria

Open any criterion to see exactly what distinguishes Strong, Meaningful, Limited, and Not a Significant Focus.

1 Literary Breadth View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial literary study across poetry, drama, and fiction, with varied authors and traditions and enough literary material for sustained interpretation, comparison, and argument.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides meaningful literary breadth across multiple major genres and can support a significant portion of the course, though some genres, voices, or traditions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides useful literary material but is narrow in genre, range, or sustained coverage. Faculty would need substantial additional literature to support the course's literary breadth.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no substantial literary content suitable for sustained C1003 study.

Calibration example: Strong = substantial poetry, drama, and fiction are used for interpretation, comparison, and argument; Limited = one genre with little material for broader literary study.
2 Diverse Literary Voices View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides a substantial range of literary voices across cultures, ethnicities, genders, sexual orientations, socioeconomic experiences, historical contexts, and/or national or global traditions, and uses that range to support comparison, argument, and examination of assumptions and context, not representation alone.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides a meaningful range of authors and perspectives beyond a narrow canonical sample, with some use of that range for comparative interpretation or argument, though additional voices or traditions may be needed for fuller breadth.

●○○ Limited alignment

Includes some diverse authors or perspectives, but representation is limited, uneven, or concentrated in a small portion of the resource, with little connection to comparative analysis or argument.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little meaningful diversity of literary voices, perspectives, cultures, or traditions.

Calibration example: Strong = varied authors and traditions are integrated throughout and used for comparative interpretation; Limited = token representation isolated from the rest of the resource.
3 Close Reading & Literary Analysis View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in annotation, explication, interpretation, literary terminology, form, style, comparison, and the use of textual evidence to develop defensible literary interpretations.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful, explicit instruction in several close-reading and literary-analysis practices and meaningful opportunities to develop interpretations with textual evidence, though some depth may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides opportunities to interpret or discuss literature but limited direct instruction in how to perform close reading or develop sustained evidence-based literary analysis.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in close reading, interpretation, explication, or literary analysis.

Calibration example: Strong = students move from annotation to a defensible, evidence-based interpretation; Limited = interpretive questions with no instruction in building the interpretation itself.
4 Argument & Thesis Development View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Explicitly and substantially teaches students to turn literary interpretation into thesis-driven, evidence-based arguments using claims, reasons, textual evidence, counterargument, organization, and rhetorical awareness.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Meaningfully teaches thesis development and several elements of literary argument, giving students useful support for constructing evidence-based interpretations, though some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Includes analytical writing or asks students to defend interpretations but provides limited direct instruction in argument structure, thesis development, reasons, evidence, or counterargument.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in constructing thesis-driven literary arguments.

Calibration example: Strong = a resource scaffolds turning an interpretation into a thesis-driven argument with counterargument; Limited = students are simply told to write a thesis.
5 Logic & Critical Reasoning View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in premises and conclusions, assumptions, induction and deduction, causation, inference, evaluating claims, and relationships among reasons and evidence, with guided application to interpretation and argument.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful, explicit instruction in several reasoning concepts and meaningful practice evaluating the logic behind claims and interpretations, though some reasoning areas may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Students may compare interpretations, defend readings, or examine assumptions, but direct instruction in logical reasoning concepts is limited. Practice opportunities alone do not constitute substantial reasoning instruction.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no direct instruction in premises, assumptions, inference, induction, deduction, causation, or evaluation of reasoning.

Calibration example: Strong = explicit reasoning concepts are taught and applied to competing interpretations or arguments; Limited = students are told to support a reading but are not taught how to evaluate reasoning.
6 Biases & Fallacies View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in cognitive biases and common fallacies, including faulty assumptions, oversimplification, weak causal claims, stereotyping, and strategies for recognizing and improving flawed reasoning.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Meaningfully teaches several biases, fallacies, or recurring reasoning problems and gives students useful practice identifying or correcting them, though some areas may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Addresses perspective, assumptions, bias, or questionable reasoning but offers limited direct instruction in named or recognizable cognitive biases and fallacies. Contextual discussion alone does not constitute substantial coverage.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in cognitive bias, fallacies, or recurring forms of faulty reasoning.

Calibration example: Strong = named fallacies and biases are explained and applied to literary argument; Limited = a general warning about “bias” with no named concepts or practice.
7 Critical Approaches & Context View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Explicitly and substantially teaches multiple literary or critical approaches and helps students use cultural, historical, theoretical, and authorial contexts to develop, compare, and evaluate interpretations.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in several critical approaches or contextual methods and meaningful opportunities to apply them, though the range or depth of approaches may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Introduces context, perspective, or one or more critical lenses but treatment is brief, primarily definitional, or inconsistently applied to literary interpretation.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in critical approaches or contextual literary analysis.

Calibration example: Strong = multiple critical lenses are explicitly taught and applied to the same or different texts; Limited = a lens is mentioned without guided application.
8 Research & Source Evaluation View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially teaches literary research and information literacy, including developing questions, locating primary and secondary sources, evaluating authority and relevance, synthesizing research, and integrating credible evidence responsibly.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides meaningful instruction in several research and source-evaluation practices and enough support for students to use secondary sources in literary argument, though some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Includes research assignments, databases, or source-use guidance but limited connected instruction in locating, evaluating, synthesizing, and using primary and secondary sources.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in literary research, information literacy, or source evaluation.

Calibration example: Strong = students move from research question to locating, evaluating, synthesizing, and integrating secondary sources; Limited = a bibliography or database link with little instruction.
9 Writing, Revision & Documentation View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially supports sustained revised formal writing through drafting, feedback, substantive revision, organization, style, quotation integration, citation, works cited or references, and academic conventions appropriate to literary studies.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Explicitly supports multiple stages of formal literary writing and provides useful documentation guidance, though some revision, style, or citation practices may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides some writing, revision, or citation support but does not substantially teach a recursive process of producing polished, documented literary arguments.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in drafting, substantive revision, formal academic writing, or documentation.

Calibration example: Strong = a full drafting-to-documentation sequence supports a revised literary argument; Limited = a formatting or citation page with little drafting or revision support.

How an AI-assisted reviewer should apply this rubric

Score each criterion independently based on explicit instructional content. For resources longer than a single unit, sample across the whole resource, including opening material, multiple body chapters or units, and later chapters, appendices, or support sections when present.

Support each rating with a brief rationale and specific evidence. When coverage is inconsistent, describe the inconsistency instead of averaging it away. The same passage may support more than one criterion.

Most important: distinguish content presence from instruction. A resource that includes literary texts, argumentative essays, citations, fallacy vocabulary, or discussion questions should not be scored as though it teaches those skills unless it provides meaningful explanation, modeling, guided practice, or scaffolding.

What this content rubric does not determine

This rubric is used to make initial, AI-assisted editorial observations that are reviewed afterward by a human editor. It is meant to help faculty decide what to investigate first—not to certify that a resource satisfies ENGL C1003 or a local course outline.

It does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any OER they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
