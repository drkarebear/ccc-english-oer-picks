ENGL C1003 Rubric

Critical Thinking and Writing through Literature

The course-specific rubric used for initial observations about how well an OER appears to integrate literary study with argument, reasoning, source evaluation, research, and revised formal writing in ENGL C1003.

How to read this rubric

C1003 brings together two kinds of work: close study of literature and critical, argumentative academic writing. The rubric therefore asks not only whether a resource contains literature, but whether it helps students reason, argue, research, and revise through literary study.

Each area is scored independently. A strong anthology may need a separate writing or reasoning resource, while a strong critical-thinking text may need substantial literary readings added.

The C1003 alignment criteria

These eight areas reflect the distinctive combination at the center of C1003: literature, close reading, critical thinking, argumentation, research, and sustained revised writing.

1

Literary Breadth

How well does the resource support substantial literary study across multiple major genres?

We look for: meaningful engagement with poetry, drama, and fiction; additional literary forms where useful; varied authors and traditions; and enough literary material to support sustained analysis.

2

Close Reading & Literary Analysis

How well does the resource help students interpret complex literary texts through attention to language, form, structure, context, and evidence?

We look for: annotation, explication, interpretation, literary terminology, textual evidence, attention to form and style, comparative analysis, and development of defensible literary interpretations.

3

Argument & Thesis Development

How well does the resource help students turn literary interpretation into clear, thesis-driven, evidence-based arguments?

We look for: arguable theses, claims, reasons, evidence, counterargument, organization, audience awareness, and sustained analytical arguments grounded in literary texts.

4

Logic & Critical Reasoning

How well does the resource teach students to examine the reasoning behind interpretations and arguments?

We look for: premises and conclusions, assumptions, inductive and deductive reasoning, causation, inference, evaluating claims, relationships among reasons and evidence, and comparing competing interpretations.

5

Biases & Fallacies

How well does the resource help students identify and avoid problems in reasoning that can weaken analysis and argument?

We look for: cognitive bias, common fallacies, faulty assumptions, oversimplification, weak causal claims, stereotyping, and strategies for evaluating and improving reasoning in students' own work.

6

Critical Approaches & Context

How well does the resource help students interpret literature using multiple critical perspectives and relevant cultural, historical, or theoretical contexts?

We look for: literary and critical approaches, contextual analysis, multiple interpretive lenses, attention to authorial and cultural context, and opportunities to compare or evaluate different readings of a text.

7

Research & Source Evaluation

How well does the resource support research, information literacy, and evaluation of secondary sources used in literary argument?

We look for: research questions, locating scholarly or credible sources, authority and relevance, source evaluation, integrating primary and secondary evidence, synthesis, and responsible use of research.

8

Writing, Revision & Documentation

How well does the resource support substantial revised academic writing and appropriate documentation in literary studies?

We look for: drafting, feedback, substantial revision, organization and style, integrating quotations, citation, works cited or references, academic conventions, and development of polished formal essays.

C1003 needs both sides of the course. A resource that is strong in literature but weak in reasoning may still be useful, just as a strong argument text may be useful without providing the literary readings. The rubric helps faculty see when a combination of OER may produce the strongest course fit.

What the dots mean in practice

Example: Argument & Thesis Development

●●●

A three-dot resource would typically provide sustained instruction and practice in developing arguable literary claims, building thesis-driven arguments, selecting evidence, addressing alternative interpretations, and organizing a complete analytical argument.

Example: Argument & Thesis Development

●○○

A one-dot resource might offer discussion questions or a brief literary analysis assignment but provide little explicit guidance in constructing and revising an argument.

The dots describe the depth and usefulness of coverage for a specific criterion, not the overall quality of the resource.

Why C1003 is not scored like C1002

C1002 and C1003 share substantial literary content, but C1003 asks students to do more explicit critical-thinking and argumentative work through literature. Its rubric therefore gives separate attention to reasoning, assumptions, biases and fallacies, argument structure, source evaluation, and sustained revision.

The distinction matters when choosing OER. A literature anthology that is an excellent fit for C1002 may still need a significant argument, reasoning, or writing supplement for C1003. Conversely, a strong critical-thinking text may need an anthology or literary core.

How these observations are produced

To make initial review manageable across a large number of OER, the project uses an AI-assisted rubric to compare resources with course-specific CCN criteria. A human editor reviews those results, while faculty recommendations and teaching notes provide an independent source of evidence that can confirm, complicate, or correct the initial assessment.

Faculty experience remains separate from editorial alignment. A resource's rubric scores describe what it appears to cover. Faculty reviews describe what instructors experienced when they actually used it.

What this rubric does not determine

This rubric does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any resource they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
