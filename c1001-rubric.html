ENGL C1001 Rubric

Critical Thinking and Writing

The course-specific rubric used for initial observations about how well an OER appears to support critical reading, argument, reasoning, source evaluation, research, and evidence-based academic writing in ENGL C1001.

How to read this rubric

This rubric is used to make initial editorial alignment observations about publicly available OER. It helps faculty compare what different resources appear to offer before deciding which ones deserve a closer look.

The categories are scored independently. A resource can be exceptionally useful for argument or logic while providing little research instruction, or vice versa. The rubric is designed to make those differences visible.

The C1001 alignment criteria

C1001 centers critical thinking and writing. The eight areas below are considered separately so faculty can distinguish a strong argument text, a logic-focused supplement, a research handbook, and a broader course spine.

1

Critical Reading

How well does the resource help students analyze, interpret, question, and evaluate complex nonfiction and argumentative texts?

We look for: active reading, identifying claims and assumptions, distinguishing reasons and evidence, interpreting context, evaluating credibility, and reading with attention to complexity and nuance.

2

Argument & Rhetoric

How well does the resource teach students to analyze and construct arguments in relation to audience, purpose, context, claims, reasons, and evidence?

We look for: argument structure, rhetorical situation, persuasive strategies, claims, reasons, evidence, counterargument, audience awareness, and rhetorical analysis.

3

Logic & Reasoning

How well does the resource help students understand and evaluate the reasoning that connects claims, evidence, and conclusions?

We look for: premises and conclusions, assumptions, inductive and deductive reasoning, validity and strength of reasoning, causal claims, analogies, inference, and evaluation of logical relationships.

4

Biases & Fallacies

How well does the resource help students recognize faulty reasoning, cognitive bias, rhetorical distortion, and common argumentative fallacies?

We look for: fallacies taught in context, cognitive biases, misleading appeals, oversimplification, weak causal reasoning, stereotyping, confirmation bias, and strategies for avoiding these problems in students' own arguments.

5

Research & Source Evaluation

How well does the resource support research as part of critical inquiry, including finding and evaluating information?

We look for: research questions, search strategies, information literacy, source credibility, authority, relevance, bias, currency, lateral reading, and evaluation of evidence before use.

6

Evidence & Documentation

How well does the resource help students select, integrate, explain, and document evidence in support of their own claims?

We look for: quotation, paraphrase, synthesis, contextualizing evidence, connecting evidence to claims, source attribution, citation, works cited or references, and ethical source use.

7

Writing & Revision

How well does the resource support thesis-driven critical writing as a process of developing, testing, refining, and revising ideas?

We look for: arguable theses, organization, development, drafting, feedback, substantial revision, counterargument, rhetorical choices, clarity, style, and reflection on the effectiveness of an argument.

8

Diverse & Complex Nonfiction

How well does the resource provide or support engagement with complex nonfiction representing varied perspectives, contexts, identities, and positions?

We look for: substantial readings or examples that give students meaningful opportunities to compare viewpoints, examine assumptions, consider social and cultural context, and practice critical reading across a range of voices.

Why there is no single “C1001 score”: a logic textbook and an argument reader may both be excellent resources while serving very different purposes. Category-by-category scoring helps faculty see what each resource can contribute and where a second text may be useful.

What the dots mean in practice

Example: Logic & Reasoning

●●●

A three-dot resource would typically provide sustained instruction in how arguments are structured and evaluated, including assumptions, premises, conclusions, inference, and multiple forms of reasoning.

Example: Logic & Reasoning

●○○

A one-dot resource might briefly mention logical appeals or common fallacies but would not provide enough reasoning instruction to carry that dimension of C1001 on its own.

The dots describe the depth and usefulness of coverage for a specific criterion, not the overall quality of the resource.

How these observations are produced

To make initial review manageable across a large number of OER, the project uses an AI-assisted rubric to compare resources with course-specific CCN criteria. A human editor reviews those results, while faculty recommendations and teaching notes provide an independent source of evidence that can confirm, complicate, or correct the initial assessment.

Faculty experience remains separate from editorial alignment. A resource's rubric scores describe what it appears to cover. Faculty reviews describe what instructors experienced when they actually used it.

What this rubric does not determine

This rubric does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any resource they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
