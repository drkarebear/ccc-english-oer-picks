ENGL C1001 Content Rubric · Content Lens

Critical Thinking and Writing

The project-developed content rubric used to examine how well an OER supports the major critical reading, argument, reasoning, research, evidence, and writing work of ENGL C1001.

What this content rubric does

This rubric is built around the distinctive work of C1001: critical reading, argument, reasoning, source evaluation, evidence use, and revision. The categories are scored independently because a logic-centered resource, an argument handbook, a research guide, and a nonfiction reader can all be useful for different reasons.

No single overall score. The goal is to make those differences visible rather than collapse them into one number.

This is the content lens. It asks whether an OER provides meaningful, teachable coverage of C1001's instructional work. It is separate from the site's ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework crosswalk, which examines inclusion, diversity, equity, and anti-racism. The two lenses are never merged into one score.

The shared four-point content-alignment scale

●●○

●○○
Limited alignment

○○○
Not a significant focus
Minimal, absent, or outside the resource's intended purpose.

How this content rubric was built

1. Start with the course.

The rubric organizes C1001's critical-reading, argument, reasoning, research, evidence, and writing work into distinct instructional domains rather than treating all critical-thinking OER as interchangeable.

2. Evaluate instruction, not keywords.

A resource does not earn strong alignment merely because it contains arguments, fallacies, sources, or complex readings. Stronger ratings require meaningful explanation, modeling, guided practice, or scaffolding.

3. Keep the evidence visible.

Reviewers support each rating with a brief rationale and a specific location—chapter, section, page, heading, or stable URL anchor whenever possible.

Working rubric: This is a faculty-developed analytical tool, not an official CCC, CCN, articulation, or curriculum determination. The rubric may be revised as evidence and faculty feedback improve the project.

The C1001 content criteria

Open any criterion to see exactly what distinguishes Strong, Meaningful, Limited, and Not a Significant Focus.

1 Critical Reading View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in analyzing, interpreting, questioning, and evaluating complex nonfiction and argumentative texts. Students learn to identify claims, assumptions, reasons, evidence, context, credibility, complexity, and nuance.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful, explicit instruction in several critical-reading practices, such as identifying claims and assumptions, evaluating evidence or credibility, or considering context. Coverage is strong enough to support instruction but may need supplementation for greater depth or range.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides some critical-reading guidance or opportunities to analyze texts, but instruction is brief, uneven, or secondary to another purpose. Faculty would need substantial additional instruction in evaluating complex nonfiction and argument.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no direct instruction in critical reading. Readings may be present, but the resource largely assumes students already know how to analyze and evaluate claims, evidence, assumptions, and context.

Calibration example: Strong = guided practice identifying claims, assumptions, evidence, and implications across multiple readings; Limited = comprehension questions without analysis of how or why a text argues.
2 Argument & Rhetoric View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Explicitly and substantially teaches students to analyze and construct arguments in relation to audience, purpose, context, claims, reasons, evidence, counterargument, and rhetorical strategy. Students apply these concepts in their own writing.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Meaningfully teaches argument and several related rhetorical concepts, such as claims, reasons, evidence, audience, purpose, counterargument, or persuasive strategies. Some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Introduces argument or rhetorical concepts, but treatment is brief, isolated, primarily definitional, or focused on a narrow formula. Limited support is provided for analyzing and constructing sustained arguments.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in argument structure, rhetorical situation, claims, reasons, evidence, counterargument, or rhetorical decision-making.

Calibration example: Strong = students build arguments from claims, reasons, and evidence while considering audience and counterargument; Limited = “argument” is defined with no construction practice.
3 Logic & Reasoning View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in how reasoning works, including premises and conclusions, assumptions, inductive and deductive reasoning, validity or strength, causal reasoning, analogy, inference, and evaluation of logical relationships.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful, explicit instruction in several reasoning concepts and gives students meaningful practice evaluating how claims, reasons, evidence, and conclusions relate. Some areas of logical reasoning may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Touches on reasoning, logical appeals, assumptions, or fallacies but does not provide sustained instruction in the structures or evaluation of reasoning. Faculty would need substantial additional logic instruction.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no direct instruction in premises, conclusions, inference, induction, deduction, causation, assumptions, or evaluation of reasoning.

Calibration example: Strong = worked examples distinguish valid from invalid inferences and inductive from deductive reasoning; Limited = a glossary of logic terms with no applied practice.
4 Biases & Fallacies View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in recognizing and avoiding cognitive biases, faulty reasoning, misleading appeals, weak causal claims, oversimplification, stereotyping, and common argumentative fallacies, with meaningful application.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Meaningfully teaches several biases, fallacies, or recurring problems in reasoning and gives students useful practice identifying or correcting them. Some areas may require supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Mentions fallacies, bias, assumptions, or misleading rhetoric, but treatment is brief, list-like, or largely incidental. Students receive limited instruction in why these problems occur or how to avoid them.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in cognitive bias, fallacies, or recurring forms of faulty reasoning.

Calibration example: Strong = named biases/fallacies are explained and applied to real arguments; Limited = a one-page list with no meaningful examples or practice.
5 Research & Source Evaluation View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially teaches research as critical inquiry, including developing questions, searching strategically, evaluating authority, credibility, relevance, bias, and currency, and making informed decisions about whether evidence is trustworthy and useful.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides meaningful instruction in several parts of research and source evaluation, such as searching, credibility, authority, relevance, or bias. Coverage is useful but may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Touches on finding or evaluating sources but offers limited connected instruction in information literacy or source evaluation. Faculty would need substantial additional research support.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in research strategies, information literacy, or evaluation of sources.

Calibration example: Strong = students evaluate authority, relevance, currency, and bias before deciding whether to use a source; Limited = a brief warning that not all websites are reliable.
6 Evidence & Documentation View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial instruction in selecting, contextualizing, integrating, explaining, synthesizing, and documenting evidence. Students learn to connect evidence to claims through quotation, paraphrase, attribution, citation, and ethical source use.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in several evidence and documentation practices and enough guidance for students to integrate and acknowledge sources effectively, though some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides brief guidance on quotation, paraphrase, citation, or evidence use but limited instruction in explaining, synthesizing, or connecting evidence to claims.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in using, integrating, explaining, or documenting evidence.

Calibration example: Strong = quotation and paraphrase choices are connected to how evidence supports a claim and paired with citation practice; Limited = a citation-formatting page with no discussion of evidence use.
7 Writing & Revision View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially supports thesis-driven critical writing as a recursive process of developing, testing, organizing, refining, and revising ideas. Includes meaningful attention to drafting, feedback, counterargument, substantive revision, rhetorical choices, clarity, and style.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Explicitly supports multiple stages of critical writing and revision and gives students meaningful opportunities to develop and refine arguments, although some stages or practices receive less attention.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides limited writing-process support, such as thesis guidance, peer review, editing, or a single revision step, but does not substantially teach the development and revision of sustained critical arguments.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Focuses primarily on finished products, correctness, or assignment requirements, with little or no meaningful instruction in developing and revising critical writing.

Calibration example: Strong = a multi-draft assignment includes feedback and substantive revision; Limited = a single editing-tips list.
8 Diverse & Complex Nonfiction View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides or meaningfully integrates a substantial range of complex nonfiction representing varied perspectives, identities, contexts, and positions, with support for comparing viewpoints and examining assumptions and social or cultural context.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides a useful range of substantive nonfiction and multiple perspectives suitable for critical reading and discussion, though breadth, complexity, or diversity may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Includes some nonfiction or varied perspectives, but selections are limited in range, complexity, diversity, or sustained instructional use.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no substantial nonfiction for critical analysis, or offers too narrow a range of voices and perspectives to support this dimension of the course.

Calibration example: Strong = a substantial range of nonfiction is used for comparison and critical analysis; Limited = a few short readings with little connection to broader critical-thinking work.

How an AI-assisted reviewer should apply this rubric

Score each criterion independently based on explicit instructional content. For resources longer than a single unit, sample across the whole resource, including opening material, multiple body chapters or units, and later chapters, appendices, or support sections when present.

Support each rating with a brief rationale and specific evidence. When coverage is inconsistent, describe the inconsistency instead of averaging it away. The same passage may support more than one criterion.

Most important: distinguish content presence from instruction. A resource that includes argumentative essays, nonfiction readings, citations, or discussion questions should not be scored as though it teaches those skills unless it provides meaningful explanation, modeling, guided practice, or scaffolding.

What this content rubric does not determine

This rubric is used to make initial, AI-assisted editorial observations that are reviewed afterward by a human editor. It is meant to help faculty decide what to investigate first—not to certify that a resource satisfies ENGL C1001 or a local course outline.

It does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any OER they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
