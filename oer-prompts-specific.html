PROMPT BANK · TARGETED SEARCH

Find Something Specific

For the moment when you do not need another textbook—you need the right reading, topic, genre, COR requirement, or teaching resource.

Search for the missing piece, not the whole course

Many faculty already have a workable course spine. The gap is smaller: a missing topic, a better set of readings, one niche COR requirement, a genre the current text barely covers, or an activity that helps students practice a difficult move.

Keep the scope honest. A strong resource for one topic can be an excellent supplement without being a good full-course textbook. These prompts are designed to find targeted materials without accidentally turning the search into a whole-course evaluation.

What are you trying to find?

A topic, concept, issue, or themeUse Find OER on One Topic.
Assign-able readingsUse Find Open Readings for a Theme.
One niche COR requirementUse Fill a COR Gap.
A missing genre or formUse Find a Genre / Modality.
An activity, exercise, or ancillaryUse Find Teaching Support.

Five different search jobs

🎯TOPIC“I need something on this.”
📖READINGS“I need texts students can read.”
🧭COR GAP“I have to cover this requirement.”
🧩GENRE“My current OER is missing this form.”
🛠️ANCILLARY“I need students to practice this.”
These searches should be narrower than a landscape scan. The AI should search deeply around the stated need, verify openness, and return resources that could realistically fill the gap—without scoring the entire course.

Prompt 1: Find OER on One Topic

Use this for a concept, issue, theme, unit, or niche subject area.

TOPIC / CONCEPT Prompt 1

Targeted OER Search

Search one topic deeply across OER repositories, adjacent terminology, and relevant open collections.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member find openly licensed material for ONE specific topic, concept, issue, theme, or unit.

I will provide:
1. the course / discipline,
2. the specific topic or concept I need,
3. optional context about the students, assignment, or unit,
4. optional notes about what I already use.

Your job is a TARGETED OER SEARCH.

This is not a full-course textbook search and not a final course-alignment evaluation.

STEP 1 — CLARIFY THE SEARCH TARGET FROM THE INFORMATION I PROVIDED

Identify:
- the exact topic / concept,
- closely related terms,
- broader and narrower terms,
- likely disciplinary synonyms,
- related controversies, subtopics, authors, genres, methods, or historical terms that could surface useful OER.

Do not ask me to narrow further unless the target is genuinely impossible to interpret.

STEP 2 — SEARCH IN LAYERS

When live web access is available, search:

A. DIRECT OER MATCHES
Look for openly licensed chapters, textbooks, modules, readings, lectures, activities, or collections explicitly focused on the target.

B. OER REPOSITORIES AND COLLECTIONS
Search relevant sources such as:
- ASCCC OERI
- LibreTexts / LibreCommons
- Pressbooks
- Open Textbook Library
- OER Commons
- MERLOT
- OASIS / Cool4Ed
- institution-hosted OER repositories
- disciplinary OER collections

C. ADJACENT SEARCH TERMS
Search related terminology when the exact phrase produces little.

D. OPEN SCHOLARLY / PUBLIC COLLECTIONS
When relevant, look for openly licensed or public-domain materials from:
- universities and libraries,
- archives,
- museums,
- government agencies,
- public humanities projects,
- reputable nonprofit or disciplinary organizations.

STEP 3 — DISTINGUISH TYPES OF RESULTS

Label each result as one of:
- textbook chapter,
- standalone reading,
- anthology / collection,
- primary source,
- module,
- lesson / activity,
- video / multimedia,
- ancillary,
- other.

STEP 4 — VERIFY OPENNESS

Do not call something OER merely because it is free to view.

For every result:
- provide the most direct link you can,
- name the host/platform,
- identify the open license when verified,
- write "License needs verification" when unclear.

If you do not have live web access, say so explicitly and do not pretend that you searched or verified resources.

STEP 5 — KEEP THE SEARCH FOCUSED

Do not evaluate the entire course.
Do not assign Content Rubric scores.
Do not apply the IDEA Framework unless I ask.
Do not pad the list with weak matches just to reach a number.

OUTPUT

1. SEARCH TARGET
Summarize the topic and the search terms you used.

2. BEST DIRECT MATCHES
Table:
Resource | Type | Author/creator | Platform | License | Why it may help | Direct link | What to verify

3. USEFUL ADJACENT MATCHES
Include resources that approach the topic through a related concept, field, genre, or context.

4. PRIMARY SOURCES / READINGS / EXAMPLES
If relevant, identify material faculty could assign directly to students.

5. TEACHING COMPONENTS
If relevant, identify openly licensed modules, activities, discussion prompts, or ancillaries that could help teach the topic.

6. GAPS
State what you could not find or verify.

7. BEST NEXT LOOKS
Identify 3–6 resources most worth a faculty member opening next.
Do not call them "best overall"; explain why each is worth a closer look.

Prompt 2: Find Open Readings for a Theme

Useful for English and other reading-centered courses where the textbook may be fine but the faculty member needs stronger, more varied, or more relevant texts to assign.

READINGS / PRIMARY SOURCES Prompt 2

Find Assignable Open Readings

Surface essays, literary works, journalism, speeches, primary sources, and other readings around a theme or question.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member find OPENLY LICENSED READINGS for a course theme, issue, question, or unit.

I will provide:
1. the course / discipline,
2. the theme, issue, or question,
3. optional preferences such as genre, length, difficulty, historical period, or perspective.

Your job is to find assignable readings or primary-source material, not full textbooks.

SEARCH FOR:
- essays,
- speeches,
- literary works,
- journalism,
- memoir / creative nonfiction,
- primary sources,
- public-domain texts,
- openly licensed scholarly or public-facing writing,
- anthology selections,
- other relevant readings.

SEARCH STRATEGY

1. Start with direct thematic terms.
2. Expand using related concepts, synonyms, controversies, historical terms, and disciplinary language.
3. Search OER repositories, Pressbooks / LibreTexts collections, institutional OER sites, libraries, archives, museums, government sources, and public-domain collections when appropriate.
4. Prefer direct links to the actual reading or stable collection page.
5. Do not assume free-to-read means openly licensed.

FOR EACH READING, PROVIDE:
- title,
- author / creator,
- date when relevant,
- genre / type,
- source / platform,
- license or public-domain status,
- approximate length if reasonably available,
- why it connects to the theme,
- what kind of classroom use it might support,
- any obvious contextual note faculty should know before assigning it.

DIVERSITY OF POSSIBILITIES

When the topic allows it, do not return ten readings that all make the same argument.
Try to surface:
- different positions,
- different genres,
- different time periods,
- different social or cultural contexts,
- different levels of complexity.

Do not infer an author's identity from a name or photo.
Only describe identity information when it is explicitly and reliably documented.

OUTPUT

1. Theme / question
2. Search terms used
3. Strong direct reading options
4. Contrasting / complementary reading options
5. Primary sources or public-domain options
6. Any notable gaps
7. A short "possible pairing" section suggesting 2–4 readings that could work well together and why

Do not claim that a reading is appropriate for every student or course.
If licensing cannot be verified, label it "License needs verification."

[genre, length, reading difficulty, period, perspective, etc.]

Prompt 3: Fill One COR Requirement

Use this when the course document contains a niche requirement your current materials do not adequately address.

NICHE COURSE REQUIREMENT Prompt 3

Search for a COR Gap

Translate one course requirement into search language, then look for the right-sized resource to address it.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member find OER for ONE specific requirement or niche area in a Course Outline of Record (COR), CCN descriptor, or C-ID descriptor.

I will provide:
1. the course document or relevant excerpt,
2. the specific requirement I am trying to cover,
3. optional information about what my current OER already covers.

Your job is to search deeply for resources that could help with THIS GAP.

STEP 1 — INTERPRET THE REQUIREMENT

Using only the course document I provide:
- explain what the requirement appears to ask students to learn or practice,
- identify key concepts and search terms,
- distinguish what is explicitly required from what you are inferring,
- generate related disciplinary terms that could help locate OER.

STEP 2 — SEARCH BEYOND THE EXACT WORDING

The wording in a COR may not match textbook titles.
Search:
- direct terminology,
- disciplinary synonyms,
- related methods / theories,
- older or alternate terminology,
- neighboring disciplines,
- chapter-level and module-level resources.

STEP 3 — SEARCH FOR THE RIGHT SIZE RESOURCE

I may not need a whole textbook.

Look for:
- textbook chapters,
- focused open textbooks,
- modules,
- readers,
- primary-source collections,
- tutorials,
- activities,
- research guides,
- ancillaries.

STEP 4 — VERIFY

For each candidate:
- direct link,
- author / creator,
- host,
- open license when verified,
- resource type,
- what part of the requirement it appears to address,
- what still needs faculty review.

Do not assign a full Content Rubric score.
Do not claim the COR requirement is satisfied.
Do not apply the IDEA Framework unless I ask.

OUTPUT

1. Requirement as written
2. Search interpretation
3. Search vocabulary
4. Direct matches
5. Adjacent / partial matches
6. Best "gap filler" candidates
7. What still appears uncovered
8. What faculty should verify before using

[PASTE OR DESCRIBE]

Prompt 4: Find a Missing Genre, Form, or Modality

This prompt deliberately separates having examples from teaching students how to work with the genre.

GENRE / FORM Prompt 4

Find Content + Instruction for a Genre

Search for assignable examples, instructional support, or resources that provide both.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member find openly licensed material in a SPECIFIC GENRE, TEXT TYPE, MODALITY, OR FORM that is missing from their course.

Examples might include:
- drama,
- poetry,
- short fiction,
- memoir,
- argument,
- visual rhetoric,
- multimodal composition,
- speeches,
- primary sources,
- case studies,
- data visualizations,
- professional genres.

I will provide:
1. the course / discipline,
2. the genre / form I need,
3. optional context about the unit or assignment.

Your job is to identify resources that provide enough of that genre or form to be genuinely useful.

SEARCH FOR BOTH:
A. CONTENT — actual examples, readings, texts, or artifacts in the genre
B. INSTRUCTION — OER that teach students how to read, analyze, interpret, or compose that genre

These are not the same thing. Keep them separate.

FOR EACH RESULT, REPORT:
- resource title,
- type,
- author / creator,
- platform,
- license status,
- whether it provides CONTENT, INSTRUCTION, or BOTH,
- what appears useful about it,
- what needs verification.

If the genre is scarce in textbook OER, broaden to:
- anthologies,
- archives,
- public-domain collections,
- institutional Pressbooks,
- modules,
- discipline-specific collections.

Do not call free online material OER unless the license or public-domain status can be verified.

OUTPUT

1. Genre / form requested
2. Search terms used
3. Best sources of assignable examples
4. Best sources of instruction
5. Resources that combine both
6. Gaps / limitations
7. Best 3–6 resources to inspect next

Prompt 5: Find an Activity or Ancillary

Use this when explanation is not enough and you need something students can actually do.

ACTIVITY / ANCILLARY Prompt 5

Find Open Teaching Support

Search for exercises, assignments, modules, quizzes, tutorials, examples, and other reusable support materials.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member find OPENLY LICENSED ANCILLARY MATERIAL for one teaching need.

I will provide:
1. the course / discipline,
2. the specific skill / concept / assignment,
3. optional information about the textbook or OER I already use.

Search for materials such as:
- practice activities,
- low-stakes exercises,
- discussion prompts,
- quizzes or question banks,
- worksheets,
- tutorials,
- interactive activities,
- assignment sequences,
- sample texts,
- instructor guides,
- slides,
- videos,
- other teachable support materials.

PRIORITIZE MATERIAL THAT:
- directly supports the stated learning need,
- is clearly reusable or adaptable,
- is openly licensed or public domain,
- can reasonably be integrated into an existing course,
- does more than merely explain the concept when practice is needed.

FOR EACH RESULT:
- title,
- creator,
- platform,
- license,
- format,
- what students actually do,
- likely time / scope when reasonably apparent,
- what part of the teaching need it addresses,
- whether it appears ready to use or would need adaptation,
- direct link.

Do not confuse a textbook chapter with an ancillary unless it contains a usable activity or teaching component.
Do not infer open licensing from free access.
If the license is unclear, write "License needs verification."

OUTPUT

1. Teaching need
2. Search terms
3. Ready-to-use ancillaries
4. Adaptable ancillaries
5. Related resources worth mining for activities
6. Gaps
7. Best 3–5 items to inspect next

Targeted-search guardrails

Targeted ≠ trivial.

A narrow search can still require multiple repositories, alternate terminology, and adjacent disciplines.

Content and instruction differ.

A resource may contain poems, arguments, or case studies without teaching students how to analyze or create them.

Free is not necessarily open.

Verify an open license or public-domain status before calling something OER.

Do not force false variety.

If the field offers only a few credible materials, report the gap rather than padding the list.

Identity claims need evidence.

Do not infer an author’s race, ethnicity, gender, disability, sexuality, or other identity from a name, photo, or appearance.

One great supplement can be enough.

The goal is to solve the teaching need, not to produce the longest possible bibliography.

Where does this fit in the bigger workflow?

A targeted search can happen almost anywhere. You might use it while building a course, after a Content Rubric exposes a gap, or after teaching with an OER and noticing something students still need.

Typical handoff: identify the gap → search specifically → verify the resource → decide whether it fills the need → evaluate more deeply only if the resource will play a major role.
