About the project

A practical faculty guide to a very large OER landscape.

CCC English OER Picks is an independent faculty project for comparing promising English OER by course, making the review criteria visible, and helping faculty decide what is worth investigating first.

Why this exists

Finding free or open materials is only the beginning. Faculty still have to decide which resources fit a particular course, what each resource actually teaches, where supplementation may be needed, and whether a promising title is worth the time required for deeper review.

CCC English OER Picks is designed to reduce some of that first-pass work without outsourcing the academic decision. The site organizes promising resources by California Community College English course and makes the criteria behind the editorial observations visible.

About the project

CCC English OER Picks is an independent faculty project created and maintained by Karen Crozer, a California Community College English faculty member. I maintain the project on a volunteer basis. It is not an official ASCCC OERI, CCCCO, CCN, articulation, or curriculum resource.

The project is intentionally revisable. Corrections, faculty teaching notes, rubric feedback, and stronger evidence can change what appears here over time.

The approach

Compare by course

C1000, C1001, C1002, and C1003 ask students to do different work, so each course uses its own rubric rather than one permanent score for a book.

Make the criteria visible

The published rubrics show what each dot rating means and what instructional coverage the review is looking for.

Use AI for first-pass labor

AI can help scan large OER, locate possible evidence, and organize draft findings. It is a research assistant here, not the final evaluator.

Keep professional judgment human

I check the source material, revise the draft ratings and notes, and make the final decision about what is published. Faculty still decide what belongs in their own courses.

How I built and use the course rubrics

I developed a separate working rubric for each course from statewide Common Course Numbering expectations and related curriculum guidance. The goal was to turn broad course expectations into a manageable set of instructional domains that could be applied consistently across very different kinds of OER.

AI helped with drafting, organizing, and testing the rubric language, but the rubric is a project-developed analytical tool rather than an official CCN instrument. I reviewed and revised the categories, descriptions, and scoring levels before using them on the site.

AI is not the evidence. A model's statement that a resource covers a criterion is not enough. The underlying OER is the evidence.

When I review an OER, I use AI for a structured first pass against the course rubric. That pass produces draft criterion ratings and possible supporting evidence. I then perform a human editorial pass: I open the resource, check whether the cited material actually supports the claim, revise ratings that are overstated or unsupported, and add practical notes about strengths, gaps, and likely course roles.

Ratings are based on explicit instructional coverage, not simply topic presence. A chapter title, reading, assignment, or vocabulary term does not automatically earn a strong score. Stronger ratings require meaningful, teachable instruction, modeling, guided practice, or sustained coverage.

Scope of the current review

I explored adding a formal IDEA-based review layer, but I am not currently publishing IDEA scores, badges, dots, or crosswalks because I cannot apply that framework consistently across every resource at the level of care it deserves. Representation, inclusion, equity, anti-racism, and accessibility may still appear in qualitative notes when there is relevant evidence, but they are not a separate scored evaluation.

Why faculty feedback matters

An editorial review can identify apparent instructional coverage, but it cannot reproduce the experience of teaching with a resource. Faculty can surface accessibility barriers, sequencing issues, assignment possibilities, representation concerns, student responses, and other realities that an initial review may miss.

Faculty recommendations and teaching notes therefore remain separate from the alignment ratings. They can confirm, complicate, or correct the editorial analysis rather than disappearing inside one blended score.

AI use and ethics

I am a co-PI on the Peer and AI Review + Reflection (PAIRR) project, which asks educators to approach generative AI critically rather than treat it as neutral or cost-free. The PAIRR ethics statement linked below was written by Julie Gamberg, Marit MacArthur, Anna Mills, Aparna Sinha, and Lisa Sperber; I did not write the statement.

Its discussion of environmental impact, labor, bias, privacy, intellectual property, voice, and human judgment informs how I use AI in this project. Here, that means using AI where it can reduce repetitive first-pass labor, protecting confidential information, checking claims against source material, and keeping professional decisions with people.

Accessibility

I aim for CCC English OER Picks to meet or exceed WCAG 2.2 Level AA for the parts of the experience controlled by this project. The site uses semantic headings and landmarks, keyboard-accessible controls, visible focus indicators, responsive reflow, accessible form labels and status messages, and reduced-motion support.

I test first-party pages for common WAVE errors and contrast problems and also review keyboard access, focus behavior, reflow, and accessible names. Automated tools cannot certify legal compliance, so accessibility remains an ongoing human review process as the site changes.

Third-party services and linked resources, including Google Groups, Supabase authentication/data services, GitHub Pages, and external OER platforms, are maintained by their own providers and may have different accessibility features or barriers.

If you encounter an accessibility barrier in this site itself, please email me about the accessibility issue and include the page and a short description of what was difficult to use.
