PROMPT BANK · DISCOVERY

Discover OER

For the moment before you have a shortlist—whether your field seems to have thirty possible books or almost none at all.

Use this bank when you are still asking, “What exists?”

Discovery should be broad enough to uncover possibilities without pretending every result is a good course fit. These prompts help AI search adaptively: organize the field when choices are plentiful, broaden intelligently when choices are scarce, and consider combinations when no single OER can do the whole job.

Discovery is not evaluation. The goal here is to uncover credible possibilities. Save Content Rubric scoring and the IDEA Framework for the resources that survive your initial screen.

Which discovery prompt should I use?

I have a course and no real shortlist.Start with Explore the OER Landscape. This is the default prompt for almost everyone.
I searched and found almost nothing.Use Search Again: Sparse Field to widen the terminology, sources, and kinds of resources being considered.
No single textbook seems to work.Use Build a Course from Several OER to explore complementary packages.
AI gave me a messy list of titles.Use Verify My Candidate List before spending time evaluating anything.

How a good discovery search expands

The search should not stop because the exact course title produced only one result.

1DirectExact or near-exact course and discipline matches.
2AdjacentRelated titles, subfields, older terms, and neighboring disciplines.
3ComponentsReaders, monographs, modules, collections, handbooks, and ancillaries.
4CombinationsTwo or three resources that may work better together than one book alone.
Search named OER collections—but do not stop there. A strong discovery pass can begin with ASCCC OERI, LibreTexts/LibreCommons, Pressbooks, Open Textbook Library, OER Commons, MERLOT, OASIS/Cool4Ed, and relevant institutional or disciplinary collections, then broaden through ordinary web search when the field is sparse.

Prompt 1: Explore the OER Landscape for My Course

This is the recommended starting point. It is deliberately adaptive: if the field is crowded, it organizes; if the field is sparse, it keeps broadening.

START HEREPrompt 1

Explore the OER Landscape

Use a course document plus a subject area to uncover direct matches, adjacent resources, and plausible combinations.

Open full prompt
You are helping a college faculty member discover open educational resources (OER) for a course before they have narrowed the field.

I will provide:
1. a Course Outline of Record (COR), Common Course Numbering (CCN) descriptor, C-ID descriptor, syllabus, or other official course description, and
2. the course subject / discipline.

Your job is to conduct a BROAD OER LANDSCAPE SEARCH.

This is discovery, not final evaluation. Do not assign course-alignment scores and do not choose a winner.

The field may be abundant, moderate, sparse, or extremely sparse. Adapt your search strategy to what you actually find.

STEP 1 — UNDERSTAND THE COURSE BEFORE SEARCHING

From the course document, identify:
- major subject areas and concepts,
- important skills or practices,
- major genres or types of material students need,
- distinctive or niche requirements,
- likely textbook labels for this course,
- useful synonyms, older/newer course names, adjacent disciplinary terms, and related search language.

Do not turn this into a full scoring rubric yet.

STEP 2 — SEARCH IN CONCENTRIC CIRCLES

Search broadly, using web search when available. Do not rely on a single repository or only the exact course title.

A. DIRECT MATCHES
Look for openly licensed books/resources explicitly designed for this course, discipline, or a very close equivalent.

B. ESTABLISHED OER COLLECTIONS
Search established OER sources when relevant and accessible, including:
- ASCCC OERI
- LibreTexts / LibreCommons
- Pressbooks Directory and institution-hosted Pressbooks networks
- Open Textbook Library
- OER Commons
- MERLOT
- OASIS / Cool4Ed or similar OER aggregators
- relevant state, system, college, university, library, or disciplinary OER collections

C. ADJACENT MATCHES
Search related course titles, subfields, older terminology, interdisciplinary labels, and resources that may cover a substantial part of the course even if the title differs.

D. COMPONENT RESOURCES
If full textbooks are scarce, look for readers and anthologies, open monographs, chapter collections, modules, primary-source collections, open course materials, openly licensed handbooks, ancillaries, and public-domain collections that could be paired with openly licensed instructional material.

E. BUILDABLE COMBINATIONS
If no single resource appears adequate, identify combinations such as core text + reader, disciplinary text + writing/research guide, anthology + instructional handbook, two complementary focused OER, or modules assembled around a smaller core resource.

STEP 3 — ADAPT TO THE SIZE OF THE FIELD

If you find MANY plausible resources:
- do not dump a huge list,
- organize the landscape,
- identify approximately 8–12 candidates most worth an initial faculty look,
- explain what distinguishes the main clusters of options.

If you find ONLY A FEW plausible resources:
- broaden terminology and adjacent fields,
- search institution-hosted and discipline-specific collections,
- look for components and combinations,
- continue until the search has reasonably exhausted credible avenues.

Do not manufacture candidates just to reach a number. If only 1–2 credible choices remain after a broad search, say so clearly.

STEP 4 — VERIFY BASIC FACTS

For every candidate:
- verify that the resource appears to exist,
- provide the most direct stable link you can find,
- identify author/editor when available,
- identify host/platform,
- identify the open license when you can verify it,
- label the resource type,
- name where you found it.

Do NOT call a resource OER merely because it is free to read. If the license cannot be verified, write: “License needs verification.”

If you do not have live web access, say so explicitly and do not pretend that you searched repositories or verified links.

STEP 5 — KEEP DISCOVERY SEPARATE FROM EVALUATION

Do not:
- assign Content Rubric scores,
- apply the IDEA Framework,
- claim the resource satisfies the COR, CCN, C-ID, articulation, or curriculum requirements,
- rank by prestige or popularity,
- declare a resource “best.”

At this stage, explain only why a resource appears worth investigating further.

OUTPUT

1. OER LANDSCAPE SNAPSHOT
Classify the landscape as Abundant / Moderate / Sparse / Very sparse and explain why in 2–4 sentences.

2. SEARCH MAP
List direct course terms, related/adjacent terms, niche terms from the course document, repositories/collections searched, and any important search avenues that could not be checked.

3. DIRECT COURSE-TEXT CANDIDATES
Table: Resource | Author/editor | Platform | License | Type | Why it surfaced | Where found | What still needs verification

4. ADJACENT OR COMPLEMENTARY RESOURCES
Use the same table structure, but clearly explain why each resource is not a direct full-course match.

5. POSSIBLE OER COMBINATIONS
If useful, suggest combinations that could plausibly cover the course more effectively than one resource alone. Do not claim the combination is fully aligned until it has been evaluated.

6. GAPS IN THE LANDSCAPE
Identify important course needs for which you found little or no promising OER.

7. MOST PROMISING NEXT LOOKS
Recommend 2–6 resources worth moving into a quick faculty screen, depending on how many credible options exist. Call them “most promising next looks,” not “best OER.”

8. SEARCH LIMITATIONS
State what could not be verified, where coverage may be incomplete, and what the faculty member should check manually.

Prompt 2: Search Again — My Field Looks Sparse

Use this after an initial search produces almost nothing. It forces a second pass through alternate terminology, institution-hosted resources, components, and adjacent disciplines instead of simply repeating the same search.

SPARSE-FIELD RESCUEPrompt 2

Second-Pass Search for a Sparse Field

Best for niche disciplines, uncommon course titles, and fields where the useful OER may be hiding outside major textbook indexes.

Open full prompt
You are helping a college faculty member who has already searched for OER and found very little.

I will provide:
1. a course description or COR / CCN / C-ID record,
2. the subject / discipline,
3. any OER or search results I already found,
4. optional notes about places I already searched.

Your job is to conduct a SECOND-PASS DISCOVERY SEARCH designed for a sparse or niche OER field.

Do not simply repeat the obvious course-title search.

STEP 1 — DIAGNOSE WHY THE SEARCH MAY BE SPARSE

Consider whether the course has several common titles, uses different disciplinary terminology, sits between disciplines, may be supported by chapter-level rather than textbook-length resources, may use older field terminology, or may work best as an assembled set.

STEP 2 — EXPAND THE SEARCH LANGUAGE

Generate and search synonyms, broader terms, narrower subtopics, adjacent disciplinary terms, historical/older terminology, likely textbook-title language, required concepts from the course document, and names of major genres, methods, periods, or practices in the field.

STEP 3 — SEARCH BEYOND THE OBVIOUS REPOSITORIES

When web access is available, search established OER repositories and aggregators, institution-hosted Pressbooks networks, university and community-college OER collections, library guides that point to actual openly licensed works, disciplinary associations or open-pedagogy projects, institutional repositories, open monographs, and relevant government, museum, archive, or nonprofit collections.

Use ordinary web search to locate OER that may not be well indexed in large repositories.

STEP 4 — LOOK FOR COMPONENTS

Search for resources that could cover one meaningful portion of the course: open textbooks in neighboring fields, anthologies/readers, primary-source collections, research/writing handbooks, topic-specific monographs, modules, and openly licensed media only when they fill a real instructional gap.

STEP 5 — CONSIDER AN ASSEMBLED OER COURSE

If no strong single textbook emerges, propose 1–3 plausible resource combinations. For each combination, explain what each component contributes, which course needs still appear uncovered, and what needs faculty verification.

GUARDRAILS
- Do not invent resources.
- Verify a direct link when possible.
- Do not call free-to-read material OER unless an open license is verified.
- If licensing is unclear, write “License needs verification.”
- Do not assign course-alignment scores yet.
- Do not apply the IDEA Framework yet.
- Do not force the search to produce a target number.
- If the landscape is genuinely sparse after a broad search, say so.
- If you do not have live web access, say so rather than pretending to search.

OUTPUT
1. Why the first search may have come up thin
2. Expanded search vocabulary
3. New direct candidates
4. New adjacent / component candidates
5. Possible assembled OER packages
6. Course needs still not covered
7. Best next places for a human to search
8. Search limitations and verification needs

Prompt 3: Build a Course from Several OER

Sometimes the important discovery is not another textbook—it is realizing that a strong course could be assembled from a small number of complementary open resources.

COMBINE RESOURCESPrompt 3

Explore an OER Package

Map likely roles, reduce redundancy, and identify 2–3 resource combinations worth evaluating more closely.

Open full prompt
You are helping a college faculty member determine whether a course could be built from MULTIPLE openly licensed resources because no single OER appears to cover the course well.

I will provide:
1. a course document or description,
2. the subject / discipline,
3. a list of OER candidates or discovery results.

Your job is to explore plausible OER combinations.

This is still a discovery / planning task, not a final course-alignment review.

STEP 1 — MAP THE COURSE NEEDS
Create a concise map of the major instructional needs visible in the course document. Group related needs together.

STEP 2 — MAP EACH CANDIDATE'S LIKELY ROLE
For each resource, identify the role it appears most likely to play based only on currently available evidence: course spine, reader/anthology, disciplinary content source, writing/research companion, focused supplement, ancillary/practice resource, or uncertain.

Do not assign dot scores.

STEP 3 — BUILD POSSIBLE PACKAGES
Propose up to 3 combinations that minimize unnecessary complexity.

For each package:
- identify the likely core resource,
- identify each companion resource,
- explain what each adds,
- identify overlap or redundancy,
- identify remaining gaps,
- flag licensing or access questions,
- estimate whether the package looks simple enough for students to navigate.

Prefer 2–3 well-chosen resources over a long pile of links when possible.

STEP 4 — DISTINGUISH “NEEDS EVALUATION” FROM “LOOKS PROMISING”
Do not claim that any package satisfies the course. Label all conclusions as preliminary until the resources are evaluated against a Content Rubric.

OUTPUT
1. Course-needs map
2. Candidate role map
3. Package A
4. Package B
5. Package C (only if meaningfully different)
6. Simplest viable option
7. Biggest unresolved gaps
8. What to evaluate next with the Content Rubric

Prompt 4: Verify My Candidate List

AI discovery can produce duplicates, stale links, unclear licenses, mirrors, or titles that are not actually OER. Clean the list before you begin serious comparison.

VERIFY BEFORE EVALUATINGPrompt 4

Verify and Clean a Candidate List

Check existence, direct links, license status, versions, duplicates, and resource type without judging course fit yet.

Open full prompt
You are helping a college faculty member clean up an AI-generated or manually assembled list of possible OER before spending time evaluating the resources.

I will provide a candidate list.

Your job is BASIC VERIFICATION AND DEDUPLICATION — not course-alignment scoring.

When live web access is available, check each candidate.

For each resource:
1. Does the title appear to exist?
2. Is the author/editor information consistent?
3. What is the most direct stable resource link?
4. What platform currently hosts it?
5. Can an open license be verified?
6. Is it a complete textbook, anthology/reader, module, monograph, ancillary, or something else?
7. Is this the current edition/version, or is there evidence of a newer one?
8. Is this a duplicate or mirror of another item on the list?
9. Is the resource meaningfully accessible for review, or is only a description / partial preview available?
10. Are there obvious red flags such as dead links, unclear licensing, misleading title similarity, or a resource that is free but not openly licensed?

RULES
- Do not infer licensing.
- “Free online” does not equal “OER.”
- If the license is not verified, write “License needs verification.”
- If you cannot access the source, say “Could not verify.”
- Do not assign Content Rubric ratings.
- Do not apply IDEA ratings.
- Do not remove a candidate merely because it looks weak for the course; this step is about factual cleanup, not fit.

OUTPUT TABLE
Candidate | Exists? | Direct link | Author/editor | Host | License status | Resource type | Current version? | Duplicate/mirror? | Verification notes

Then provide:

KEEP FOR SCREENING
Candidates that appear real, accessible, and sufficiently verifiable to inspect further.

VERIFY BEFORE USING
Candidates with unresolved link, license, version, or access questions.

REMOVE AS DISCOVERY NOISE
Only candidates that appear nonexistent, duplicated without benefit, clearly misidentified, or not actually OER.

If you do not have live web access, say so and limit the response to identifying what needs verification.

Discovery guardrails worth keeping visible

Free is not necessarily open.

A resource should not be labeled OER unless its open license can be verified.

Do not force a long list.

If a niche field genuinely has only one or two credible choices, that is a useful finding—not a failure.

Search terms matter.

Course catalog titles often differ from the language used by authors, disciplines, and OER repositories.

Titles are not evidence of fit.

A promising title earns a closer look, not a Content Rubric score.

Components count.

A reader, monograph, or focused guide may be valuable even when it cannot function as the whole course text.

Say what could not be searched.

If web access, full text, licensing, or a repository could not be verified, the AI should say so explicitly.

What comes after discovery?

Once you have a credible pool, do not deeply score all of it. Use a quick screen to keep the resources that seem genuinely plausible, then move only the serious candidates into deeper review.

A typical handoff: broad discovery → manageable shortlist → Content Rubric → IDEA Framework → compare finalists → faculty decision.
