Compare Finalists

Bring Content findings, IDEA findings, supplementation needs, resource roles, and your own teaching priorities together without turning them into a hidden leaderboard.

Compare tradeoffs, not just scores

The final step should help you understand what each resource would mean in an actual course: what it teaches well, what it leaves for you to supply, how the separate IDEA findings complicate the picture, and what students would have to navigate.

No hidden leaderboard. The goal is a defensible faculty decision tied to course needs and teaching priorities—not an AI-generated winner detached from context.

Prompt 1: Compare My Finalists

Bring together the evidence you already gathered and connect it to your own course priorities.

FINAL SYNTHESISPrompt 1

Compare Finalists by Evidence and Priorities

Surface meaningful tradeoffs while keeping Content and IDEA findings distinct.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member compare 2–4 OER finalists after separate Content Rubric and IDEA Framework reviews have already been completed.

I will provide, for each finalist:
- title and link,
- intended or likely role,
- completed Content Rubric findings,
- completed IDEA Framework crosswalk findings,
- known access, licensing, accessibility, currency, or format concerns,
- my teaching priorities and non-negotiables.

Your job is to SYNTHESIZE the existing evidence, not silently rescore the books.

RULES
1. Keep Content and IDEA findings visibly separate.
2. Do not create one overall numeric score unless I explicitly provide a faculty-designed scoring method.
3. Do not treat every resource as though it must be an all-in-one textbook.
4. Distinguish evidence from judgment.
5. Surface uncertainty and missing information.
6. Do not call a resource "best" unless the stated faculty priorities clearly justify that conclusion.
7. Identify where pairing resources may solve a gap better than choosing a single text.

OUTPUT
A. Side-by-side snapshot
For each finalist, summarize:
- strongest Content Alignment areas,
- most important content gaps / supplementation needs,
- strongest IDEA findings,
- most important IDEA concerns / opportunities,
- likely role,
- practical implementation issues,
- evidence confidence.

B. Tradeoffs that matter
Identify the 5–8 differences most likely to affect an instructor's decision.

C. Match to my priorities
For each priority I provide, explain which finalist appears strongest, why, and what uncertainty remains.

D. Supplementation map
Show what each finalist would still need if adopted as the primary or core resource.

E. Decision questions
List the questions I should answer myself before choosing.

F. Provisional recommendation
Offer a recommendation only if the evidence and my stated priorities support one. Otherwise, explain why the remaining choice is genuinely judgment-dependent.

Prompt 2: Test a Core + Supplement Pairing

Use this when two narrower resources may create a stronger course than one supposedly comprehensive text.

RESOURCE PAIRINGPrompt 2

Compare Single-Text and Paired Options

Look for complementary strengths without ignoring duplication, workload, or platform burden.

Open full prompt 
You are helping a college faculty member test whether a PAIRING of OER would work better than any single finalist alone.

I will provide:
- 2–4 OER finalists,
- their Content Rubric findings,
- their separate IDEA findings,
- known strengths, gaps, licenses, formats, and implementation concerns,
- my course priorities.

Evaluate plausible pairings such as:
- core text + targeted supplement,
- literature reader + writing / research support,
- argument text + anthology,
- primary text + ancillary activities.

RULES
- Do not reward a pairing merely because it contains more material.
- Look for complementary strengths that address documented gaps.
- Flag duplication, student workload, navigation burden, accessibility complexity, and mixed licensing.
- Keep Content and IDEA findings separate.
- Prefer the simplest workable combination when two options solve the same need.

OUTPUT
1. Most promising pairings.
2. What gap each pairing solves.
3. What duplication or new burden it creates.
4. Likely student experience: number of platforms, navigation, reading load, and coherence.
5. Remaining supplementation needs.
6. Best single-resource option for comparison.
7. Recommendation: single resource or pairing, with reasons tied to my stated priorities.

Prompt 3: Stress-Test My Provisional Choice

Run this after you have a likely choice but before you stop looking. It is designed to catch the concern that becomes obvious only after adoption.

DECISION CHECKPrompt 3

Skeptical Adoption Review

Test the strongest case for and against your provisional choice.

Open full prompt 
You are a skeptical second reviewer helping a college faculty member stress-test a provisional OER adoption decision.

I will provide:
- the finalists and their evidence-based Content and IDEA findings,
- my provisional choice,
- my reasons for choosing it,
- any planned supplements.

Your job is NOT to argue against my choice automatically. Your job is to find the strongest reasons the decision could be wrong, incomplete, or harder to implement than it appears.

CHECK FOR
- a major course-content gap I minimized,
- IDEA concerns I treated as cosmetic,
- a resource role mismatch,
- dependence on supplements that creates too much workload or fragmentation,
- access, licensing, accessibility, currency, or platform issues,
- evidence that was weak or based on partial access,
- a finalist I dismissed for reasons not supported by the evidence,
- hidden assumptions about my students or teaching context.

OUTPUT
1. Strongest case for my provisional choice.
2. Strongest case against it.
3. Three things I should verify directly before adopting.
4. What would make the decision safer or stronger.
5. Whether another finalist or pairing deserves reconsideration.
6. Final assessment: Well supported / Reasonable with cautions / Needs more evidence.

Final-comparison guardrails

Do not merge the lenses.

Content Alignment and IDEA findings can sit beside one another without becoming one total score.

Do not confuse breadth with fit.

A larger book is not automatically a better teaching choice.

Count supplementation honestly.

A resource can look stronger on paper than it feels once you add three extra platforms and six missing units.

Keep uncertainty visible.

If a finalist was only partially accessible, that limitation belongs in the decision.

Use your own priorities.

AI should organize tradeoffs, not invent the values that decide your course.

Faculty decide.

The best final output is a clear decision aid, not an automated adoption verdict.

End with a teaching decision you can explain

A useful final comparison should leave you able to say why a resource fits your course, what it does not do, what you will supplement, and what evidence shaped the decision.

Final handoff: evidence → tradeoffs → teaching priorities → adoption role → supplementation plan → faculty decision.
