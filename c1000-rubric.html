ENGL C1000 Rubric

Academic Reading and Writing

The course-specific rubric used for initial observations about how well an OER appears to support the major reading, writing, rhetoric, research, and language work of ENGL C1000.

How to read this rubric

This rubric is used to make initial editorial alignment observations about publicly available OER. It is meant to help faculty decide what to investigate first—not to certify that a resource satisfies ENGL C1000 or a local course outline.

Each criterion is scored independently on the same 0–3 scale used throughout CCC English OER Picks. A resource does not need to earn three dots in every category to be useful.

The C1000 alignment criteria

The seven areas below are considered separately. The goal is to show what kind of work a resource can help faculty teach, rather than reduce an OER to one overall score.

1

Academic Reading

How well does the resource support active, critical, and purposeful reading of college-level texts?

We look for: reading strategies, annotation or inquiry, analysis of ideas and evidence, attention to context and purpose, and opportunities for students to engage with challenging academic or public texts.

2

Rhetoric & Rhetorical Awareness

How well does the resource help students understand writing as a rhetorical act shaped by audience, purpose, context, genre, and choices?

We look for: rhetorical situation, audience awareness, purpose, appeals and strategies, analysis of how texts work, and transfer of rhetorical decision-making into students' own writing.

3

Writing Process

How well does the resource support writing as a recursive process rather than only presenting rules for a finished product?

We look for: invention, planning, drafting, feedback, revision, editing, reflection, metacognition, and opportunities to make substantial changes during the development of a piece of writing.

4

Academic Genres

How well does the resource help students read, analyze, and compose sustained college-level writing in multiple rhetorical or academic situations?

We look for: meaningful genre instruction, models, rhetorical flexibility, thesis-driven and analytical writing, synthesis, argument, response, and assignments that move beyond a single formula.

5

Research & Source Use

How well does the resource support finding, evaluating, integrating, and using sources as part of academic inquiry and writing?

We look for: research questions, search strategies, source evaluation, information literacy, synthesis, quotation and paraphrase, integrating evidence, and responsible use of sources.

6

Documentation

How well does the resource teach students to acknowledge and document sources using appropriate academic conventions?

We look for: in-text citation, works cited or references, attribution, avoiding plagiarism through ethical source use, and practical guidance for applying documentation conventions in student writing.

7

Language, Style & Rhetorical Choice

How well does the resource treat language and style as meaningful choices connected to rhetorical situation, clarity, effectiveness, and voice?

We look for: sentence-level choices, style, tone, voice, clarity, editing, linguistic flexibility, audience expectations, and an approach to language that goes beyond simply labeling usage as correct or incorrect.

Why there is no single “C1000 score”: two resources can be equally useful for very different reasons. One might provide excellent writing-process and rhetoric instruction while another offers stronger research, documentation, or readings. The category-by-category view makes those differences visible.

What the dots mean in practice

Example: Research & Source Use

●●●

A three-dot resource would typically provide substantial instruction in developing research questions, locating and evaluating sources, integrating evidence, and synthesizing source material into students' own writing.

Example: Research & Source Use

●○○

A one-dot resource might briefly discuss using quotations or evaluating websites but would not provide enough research instruction to carry that part of the course on its own.

The same principle is applied to each criterion: the dots describe the depth and usefulness of coverage, not the overall quality of the OER.

How these observations are produced

To make initial review manageable across a large number of OER, the project uses an AI-assisted rubric to compare resources with course-specific CCN criteria. A human editor reviews those results, while faculty recommendations and teaching notes provide an independent source of evidence that can confirm, complicate, or correct the initial assessment.

Faculty experience remains separate from editorial alignment. A resource's rubric scores describe what it appears to cover. Faculty reviews describe what instructors experienced when they actually used it. Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

What this rubric does not determine

This rubric is an independent faculty-project tool. It does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor or student population.

Faculty should still review any OER they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, institutional requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and teaching goals.
