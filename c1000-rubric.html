ENGL C1000 Content Rubric · Content Lens

Academic Reading and Writing

The project-developed content rubric used to examine how well an OER supports the major reading, writing, rhetoric, research, documentation, and language work of ENGL C1000.

What this content rubric does

This rubric is built around the instructional work of first-year composition rather than around the features of any one textbook. The categories are scored independently because an OER may be especially strong in rhetoric and writing process while another may be stronger in research, documentation, or academic reading.

No single overall score. The goal is to show what each resource can help faculty teach—not to reduce it to one number.

This is the content lens. It asks whether an OER provides meaningful, teachable coverage of C1000's instructional work. It is separate from the site's ASCCC OERI IDEA Framework crosswalk, which examines inclusion, diversity, equity, and anti-racism. The two lenses are never merged into one score.

●●○

●○○
Limited alignment

○○○
Not a significant focus
Minimal, absent, or outside the resource's intended purpose.

How this content rubric was built

1. Start with the course.

The rubric organizes C1000's instructional work into a manageable set of content areas rather than evaluating a resource by reputation, table of contents, or a generic composition checklist.

2. Evaluate instruction, not keywords.

A reading, assignment, citation, or topic does not earn a strong score simply because it appears. Stronger alignment requires meaningful explanation, modeling, guided practice, or scaffolding.

3. Keep the evidence visible.

Reviewers support each rating with a brief rationale and a specific location—chapter, section, page, heading, or stable URL anchor whenever possible.

Working rubric: This is a faculty-developed analytical tool, not an official CCC, CCN, articulation, or curriculum determination. The rubric may be revised as evidence and faculty feedback improve the project.

The C1000 content criteria

Open any criterion to see exactly what distinguishes Strong, Meaningful, Limited, and Not a Significant Focus.

1 Academic Reading View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in active and critical reading of college-level texts, including annotation, questioning, summarizing, identifying claims and evidence, and analyzing structure and context, with enough guided practice that students could develop academic reading skill primarily from this resource.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful, explicit instruction in several reading practices, such as annotation, identifying main ideas, analyzing evidence, or considering context. Coverage is strong enough to support instruction, though range or depth may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides some reading-related content or apparatus, such as discussion questions or a brief strategy sidebar, but treatment is brief, secondary to another purpose, or not sustained enough to teach reading strategies directly.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no direct instruction in academic reading strategies; assumes students already know how to read critically, or reading is incidental to another purpose.

Calibration example: Strong = guided annotation plus evaluation of claims/evidence across multiple texts; Limited = a single reading-tip sidebar with no guided practice.
2 Rhetoric & Rhetorical Awareness View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Explicitly and substantially teaches the rhetorical situation, including audience, purpose, context, genre, and rhetorical choices, with opportunities to analyze texts rhetorically and apply that awareness to students' own writing.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Meaningfully teaches several rhetorical concepts, such as audience, purpose, appeals, context, or genre, and gives useful practice, though some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Introduces rhetorical vocabulary or concepts briefly or in passing but does not sustain instruction in rhetorical analysis or application.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in rhetorical situation, audience, purpose, context, genre, or rhetorical decision-making.

Calibration example: Strong = students analyze how audience and purpose shape a real text and then apply that analysis to their own draft; Limited = “audience” is defined with no application.
3 Writing Process View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially supports writing as a recursive process, including invention, planning, drafting, feedback, revision, editing, and reflection, with meaningful opportunities for substantial change during development rather than only correctness at the end.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Explicitly supports multiple stages of the writing process and gives useful opportunities to draft, revise, and reflect, though some stages receive less attention.

●○○ Limited alignment

Provides limited process support, such as a single revision checklist, peer-review activity, or editing tips, but does not substantially teach invention, drafting, feedback, and revision as connected stages.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Focuses primarily on finished products or correctness, with little or no meaningful instruction in developing or revising writing.

Calibration example: Strong = an assignment sequence includes invention, drafting, feedback, and substantive revision; Limited = only an end-stage editing checklist.
4 Academic Genres View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in reading, analyzing, and composing sustained college-level writing across multiple genres or rhetorical situations, with models, guided practice, and assignments that move beyond a single formula.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in one or more academic genres with models and guided practice, though the range of genres or depth of instruction may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Introduces a genre or writing task, such as a fixed essay template, with limited models or guided instruction in composing it.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful genre instruction or models for sustained academic writing.

Calibration example: Strong = several genre units include models and scaffolded assignments; Limited = one formulaic template with little discussion of genre or rhetorical situation.
5 Research & Source Use View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in research as inquiry, including developing research questions, locating and evaluating sources, integrating evidence, synthesizing source material, and using sources responsibly in students' own writing.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in several parts of the research process, such as search strategies, evaluating credibility, integrating quotations, or synthesis, sufficient to support instruction though some dimensions may need supplementation.

●○○ Limited alignment

Briefly addresses an isolated research practice, such as quotation, evaluating websites, or finding sources, but does not provide enough connected instruction to support academic research independently.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in research questions, source-finding strategies, source evaluation, integration, or synthesis.

Calibration example: Strong = students move from research question to source search, evaluation, integration, and synthesis; Limited = a brief reminder to use quotations or evaluate websites.
6 Documentation View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Provides substantial, explicit instruction in a recognized documentation style, including in-text citation, works cited or references, attribution practices, and ethical source use, with practical models students can apply.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in citation and attribution practices sufficient for students to apply a documentation style with some guidance, though coverage of details or edge cases may be incomplete.

●○○ Limited alignment

Mentions citation or plagiarism briefly, such as a single page or checklist, without walkthroughs or models sufficient to teach a documentation style.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in citation, attribution, works cited or references, or documentation conventions.

Calibration example: Strong = a full MLA or APA sequence includes in-text citation, works cited/references, examples, and practice; Limited = a short reminder to “cite your sources.”
7 Language, Style & Rhetorical Choice View rating descriptions
●●● Strong alignment

Substantially treats language and style as rhetorical choices connected to clarity, voice, tone, audience, and purpose, with guided practice in sentence-level revision and an approach to usage that goes beyond correctness rules.

●●○ Meaningful alignment

Provides useful instruction in several style or language topics, such as conciseness, tone, sentence variety, or clarity, connected to rhetorical effect, though coverage may be uneven.

●○○ Limited alignment

Addresses grammar or usage primarily as correctness rules, with limited attention to style as a rhetorical or audience-driven choice.

○○○ Not a significant focus

Provides little or no meaningful instruction in style, voice, sentence-level choice, or language as rhetorical action.

Calibration example: Strong = sentence-level revision is explicitly connected to audience, clarity, tone, and effect; Limited = a grammar-rules chapter with no rhetorical connection.

How an AI-assisted reviewer should apply this rubric

Score each criterion independently based on explicit instructional content. For resources longer than a single unit, sample across the whole resource, including opening material, multiple body chapters or units, and later chapters, appendices, or support sections when present.

Support each rating with a brief rationale and specific evidence. When coverage is inconsistent, describe the inconsistency instead of averaging it away. The same passage may support more than one criterion.

Most important: distinguish content presence from instruction. A resource that includes argumentative essays, citations, discussion questions, or research assignments should not be scored as though it teaches those skills unless it provides meaningful explanation, modeling, guided practice, or scaffolding.

What this content rubric does not determine

I use this rubric to guide an initial AI-assisted review, then check the evidence and ratings myself before publishing the editorial analysis. It is meant to help faculty decide what to investigate first—not to certify that a resource satisfies ENGL C1000 or a local course outline.

It does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any OER they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
