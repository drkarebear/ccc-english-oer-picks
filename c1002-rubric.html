ENGL C1002 Rubric

Introduction to Literature

The course-specific rubric used for initial observations about how well an OER appears to support literary study across genres, close reading, diverse voices, literary terminology, analysis, and research-based writing in ENGL C1002.

How to read this rubric

C1002 is fundamentally a literature course. This rubric therefore focuses first on the breadth and quality of opportunities students have to read, interpret, discuss, and write about literary works.

Each area is scored independently. A resource may offer outstanding literary analysis instruction but contain little drama, or provide an excellent anthology while needing supplemental guidance for research and writing.

The C1002 alignment criteria

These eight areas reflect the major work of an introductory literature course: encountering multiple genres and voices, learning how literary texts work, practicing close reading and interpretation, and developing formal analysis.

1

Literary Breadth

How well does the resource support a broad introduction to literary study rather than concentrating on only one narrow genre, period, author, or theme?

We look for: substantial opportunities to study multiple literary genres, varied forms and traditions, and enough range to function as a meaningful part of an introductory literature course.

2

Poetry

How well does the resource support the study and interpretation of poetry?

We look for: a meaningful selection of poems, poetic form, imagery, figurative language, sound, rhythm, voice, structure, and opportunities for close reading and interpretation.

3

Drama

How well does the resource support the study of dramatic literature?

We look for: complete plays or substantial dramatic texts, dramatic structure, character, dialogue, performance context, genre conventions, and opportunities to analyze how drama creates meaning.

4

Fiction

How well does the resource support the study of short fiction and, where applicable, longer fictional forms?

We look for: meaningful fiction selections and instruction involving plot, character, point of view, setting, symbolism, theme, structure, and other elements useful for interpretation and analysis.

5

Diverse Literary Voices

How well does the resource expose students to authors and literary traditions representing a meaningful range of cultures, identities, perspectives, and contexts?

We look for: breadth across culture, ethnicity, gender, sexuality, socioeconomic experience, historical context, national or global traditions, and perspectives that extend beyond a narrow canonical sample.

6

Literary Terms & Critical Approaches

How well does the resource teach the concepts and vocabulary students need to discuss how literary texts create meaning?

We look for: literary terminology, genre conventions, formal elements, literary devices, and accessible introduction to interpretive or critical approaches that students can apply to texts.

7

Close Reading & Literary Analysis

How well does the resource help students move from observation to interpretation and support literary claims with textual evidence?

We look for: annotation, close reading, explication, interpretation, attention to form and language, analytical questions, use of textual evidence, and sustained literary analysis.

8

Research & Writing About Literature

How well does the resource support formal writing about literature, including appropriate use of primary and secondary sources?

We look for: literary analysis essays, thesis development, integrating quotations from primary texts, secondary research, source evaluation, documentation, drafting or revision, and conventions of academic literary writing.

A resource does not need to do everything. C1002 often works especially well with combinations: an anthology or collection of literary texts can be paired with a shorter guide to literary analysis, research, or writing. The rubric is designed to make those complementary strengths visible.

What the dots mean in practice

Example: Drama

●●●

A three-dot resource would typically include substantial dramatic literature and enough instructional support for students to study dramatic form, conventions, language, character, structure, and interpretation.

Example: Drama

●○○

A one-dot resource might mention dramatic terminology or include a single brief example, but faculty would likely need to add substantial dramatic readings and instruction.

The dots describe the depth and usefulness of coverage for that category, not whether the resource is good or bad overall.

Why C1002 is scored differently from C1003

C1002 and C1003 both use literature, but they are not interchangeable. C1002 centers the study, interpretation, and analysis of literature across major genres. C1003 adds a stronger critical-thinking and argumentation framework, including explicit attention to reasoning, bias and fallacies, thesis-driven argument, source evaluation, and revised formal writing.

In other words: an excellent C1002 resource should not lose points simply because it is not a logic or argument textbook. The C1002 rubric is intentionally built around literature first.

How these observations are produced

To make initial review manageable across a large number of OER, the project uses an AI-assisted rubric to compare resources with course-specific CCN criteria. A human editor reviews those results, while faculty recommendations and teaching notes provide an independent source of evidence that can confirm, complicate, or correct the initial assessment.

Faculty experience remains separate from editorial alignment. A resource's rubric scores describe what it appears to cover. Faculty reviews describe what instructors experienced when they actually used it.

What this rubric does not determine

This rubric does not determine official CCN compliance, local curriculum approval, articulation, accessibility compliance, licensing compliance, or suitability for every instructor, assignment, or student population.

Faculty should review any resource they plan to adopt in relation to their own course outline, local requirements, accessibility expectations, students, assignments, and instructional goals.
